In this toy model the cloud is cheap in energy but far away, so it is slow. Fog nodes are fast but spend more energy and slow down when you pile tasks onto one of them. That tension is exactly the one in Paper 1 and in PCNME: two goals that fight each other.

Why one best answer does not exist

With one goal, you just pick the smallest number. With two goals, plan A might use less energy while plan B has less delay. Neither is better. NSGA-II needs a rule to compare plans, and that rule is dominance.

A dominates B if A is no worse on both goals and strictly better on at least one.

Click any plan below. Plans it dominates turn red: they sit up and to the right, worse on both goals. Plans that dominate it turn green. Everything else is a trade-off: better on one goal, worse on the other.

The plans nobody dominates form the Pareto front. Every point on it is a legitimate answer. Choosing one of them is a human decision about how much you value energy against delay, not a math question.

How a plan is written down: the chromosome

A genetic algorithm needs every solution encoded as a list. Here each position is a task and each value is where that task runs. Paper 1 does the same, with one gene per IoT request. PCNME does it per pebble step.

Crossover builds a child from two parents: each gene is copied from one parent or the other. The white bar marks genes taken from parent A. Mutation randomly moves one task to a different node, so the search can reach assignments neither parent had.

Numbers are node IDs: 1 to 4 are fog nodes, 5 is the cloud.

Sorting the population into fronts

This is the first half of NSGA-II's name: non-dominated sorting. Find everyone nobody beats: that is front 1. Remove them. Among the rest, find everyone nobody beats: front 2. Repeat until the population is empty. A plan's front number is its rank, and lower is better.

Paper 1's Algorithm 2 is the fast version from Deb (2002). Instead of re-checking everyone each round, it records once who dominates whom, then peels the fronts using counters. Same result, much faster.

All plans are unsorted.

Keeping the front spread out: crowding distance

Rank alone is not enough. If all survivors bunch up in one corner, you only learn one kind of trade-off. So within a front, NSGA-II prefers plans that sit in empty regions. For each plan, take its two neighbours on the front and measure the box between them, normalised by the front's range on each goal. A bigger box means lonelier and more valuable. The two end points get infinity so the extremes are never lost.

The full loop

Now the pieces fit together. One generation, which is what happens every time you press "Next generation" at the top:

  1. Start with N random plans and compute energy and delay for each.
  2. Pick parents by tournament. Grab two random plans. The lower front wins. If they share a front, the larger crowding distance wins.
  3. Make N children with crossover and mutation, then evaluate them.
  4. Merge parents and children into one pool of 2N.
  5. Sort the pool into fronts.
  6. Refill the next population front by front. When a front does not fit completely, take its members with the largest crowding distance.
  7. Repeat until the generation limit. Front 1 of the final population is your answer.

Step 4 is why NSGA-II is called elitist: parents compete with their own children, so a good plan can never be lost. That, fast sorting and crowding distance are the three ideas Deb added in 2002. Everything else is a normal genetic algorithm.

Constraints such as deadlines and memory are usually handled by a simple rule: a plan that breaks a constraint always loses to a plan that does not. Paper 1 does something cruder: it sets the rank of an infeasible plan to infinity so it dies immediately.

Where Paper 1 is weaker than it looks

They never clearly say how MMDE plugs into NSGA-II

Algorithms 8 to 11 describe MMDE as its own loop with a single fitness value per individual. A min-heap needs one number to order by, while NSGA-II has two objectives. The paper does not explain where MMDE replaces or adds to the NSGA-II steps. If you ever claim to reproduce "NSGA-II-MMDE", you will have to decide this yourself.

Their Pareto front comes from a weighted sum

Table 7 builds the front by sweeping weights from 0 to 1, eleven single-objective runs. That is exactly the work NSGA-II is meant to avoid, since one run already gives the whole front.

The statistics are read backwards

The ANOVA gives significance 0.417, above 0.05, and they write that the alternative hypothesis is accepted. The correct reading is the opposite: there is no significant difference in energy between the methods. Do not cite their energy comparison as proven.

Small errors in the pseudo-code

In Algorithm 2 the front-peeling loop sits inside the first loop instead of after it. Algorithm 4 is called "tournament" but is one-point crossover; tournament is a selection method. Harmless for understanding, but do not copy them.

Your PCNME optimizer

The skeleton is right: integer chromosome, non-dominated sorting, crowding distance, knee point. The problems are in the details, and some are real bugs.

Arithmetic on node IDs means nothing

Your mutation computes r1 + F × (r2 − r3) on gene values. Genes are node labels, not quantities. "Fog 4 minus fog 1" is not a direction in any space, so this is effectively a biased random reassignment. Use a categorical operator instead, for example: copy the gene from r2 where r2 and r3 disagree, otherwise keep r1's gene. Or use plain random reassignment plus crossover.

This is not MMDE

What you wrote is standard DE/rand/1 with binomial crossover. There is no min-heap, no bottom-boosting and no partial regeneration. Call it "DE-enhanced NSGA-II", or implement GDE3, the standard multi-objective DE. The pymoode package offers it, but check the current version before relying on it.

Your selection rule contradicts itself

The text says the trial replaces the parent "if it is not dominated". The formula says "if it dominates the parent, or equals it". Those are different rules. On top of that, step 14 merges again and re-sorts, which is a second, different selection. Pick one scheme: GDE3's rule (trial dominates: replace; parent dominates: keep; neither: keep both, then trim by sorting and crowding), or classic NSGA-II with tournament, children, merge and truncate.

No parent selection and no constraint handling

r1, r2 and r3 are drawn uniformly, so rank and crowding never influence who breeds. You also never say what happens to a plan that breaks a deadline or a fog capacity. State the constrained-dominance rule explicitly.

The latency objective does not match the problem

Your problem defines task latency along the DAG's critical path, but the optimizer sums the latency of every step. And fog load is frozen per batch, so placing twenty steps on the same fog costs the same as placing one. The optimizer will pile everything on the least-loaded node, and the capacity constraint is the only thing stopping it. Make load grow with assignment, as in the toy model at the top of this page.

What is already good

Knee-point selection is a cleaner choice than Paper 1's weight sweep. Hypervolume is the right quality metric for the front. The complexity analysis is correct. Keeping the whole front instead of only the knee, which your supervisor asked for, is the remaining big fix.

What you should be able to say without notes

What dominance means, and why two objectives give a front instead of one answer. How the chromosome encodes a plan. Why sorting gives ranks and why crowding keeps diversity. What elitism is and where it happens in the loop. How constraints are enforced. Why NSGA-II runs offline in PCNME: it needs thousands of evaluations per decision, while the DQN needs one forward pass. If you can explain those six things plainly, you understand NSGA-II better than most papers that use it.
